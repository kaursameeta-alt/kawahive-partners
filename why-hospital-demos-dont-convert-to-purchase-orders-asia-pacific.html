Why Aren't My Hospital Demos Converting into Purchase Orders in Asia-Pacific?

For Series A–C MedTech, medical device and IVD founders expanding into Asia-Pacific.

Hospital demos create clinical interest. Purchase orders require a commercial pathway. This Insight shares lessons from MedTech market entry across Asia-Pacific about hospital procurement, distributor strategy and customer adoption.

← Back to KawaHive Insights

The hospital demo went well.

Clinicians were engaged. The product performed. Someone said it was exactly what they needed.

Then weeks passed. Follow-ups went unanswered. The champion became harder to reach. No purchase order appeared.

This pattern is familiar to many Series A–C MedTech founders working on hospital procurement and customer adoption across Asia-Pacific.

A successful hospital demo proves technical capability and clinical interest. It does not automatically create a purchase order. Converting interest into a buying decision requires a commercial pathway that accounts for hospital buying processes, budget cycles, distributor strategy and the full decision chain.

Related reading: Why MedTech Products Don't Sell After Regulatory Approval explores the broader commercial gaps that appear after regulatory clearance. The same principle applies after a strong demo — clinical progress is not the same as commercial progress.

What a market-entry project in Nepal taught us

In one MedTech market-entry project in Nepal, the clinical response to the product was positive. Demonstrations generated genuine interest from clinicians who could see workflow and patient benefits.

Yet conversion stalled.

The gap was not clinical performance. It was the distance between clinical enthusiasm and the hospital buying process. Procurement timing, budget ownership, service expectations and the role of local distributors had not been mapped with the same care as the clinical messaging.

That experience is common across Asia-Pacific. Hospital demos are necessary. They are rarely sufficient on their own.

Commercial question to ask

After a successful demo, what is the realistic path from clinical interest to a signed purchase order in this specific hospital or hospital group?

When clinical evidence feels like progress

Founders and clinical teams often treat a strong demo as evidence of market traction. In reality it is evidence of clinical engagement.

Hospital procurement in Asia-Pacific typically involves multiple stakeholders beyond the clinicians in the room:

  • Department heads and clinical champions
  • Biomedical engineering or materials management
  • Procurement and finance teams
  • Hospital administration or group purchasing structures
  • Local distributors who influence access and ongoing support

Each group evaluates medical devices and IVD products through a different lens. Clinical outcomes matter. So do total cost of ownership, training burden, service reliability, reimbursement fit and budget timing.

When the demo only answers clinical questions, the rest of the buying committee still has unanswered objections. Interest remains interest.

Commercial question to ask

Who else needs to say yes after the clinical champion is convinced — and have we given them a reason to support the purchase?

A good demo is only the beginning

Many demos end with polite interest and a vague commitment to “discuss internally.” Without a defined next step — evaluation protocol, pilot agreement, budget request process, tender timeline or formal commercial proposal — momentum fades quickly inside busy hospital environments.

Customer adoption depends on a structured post-demo pathway. That pathway must fit the local hospital buying process, the fiscal calendar and the practical role of distributors in that market.

Distributor strategy is especially important across Asia-Pacific. In many countries the distributor is not only a logistics partner but a key influence on hospital access, training and ongoing confidence in the product. A demo that impresses clinicians but leaves the distributor relationship unclear often fails to progress to purchase orders.

Commercial question to ask

What specific next commercial action was agreed after the demo, and who owns it on both the hospital and supplier side?

Before you commit to the next step

Before investing further time or resources in additional hospital demos, it is worth clarifying several commercial fundamentals:

  • Do we understand the hospital’s budget cycle and capital approval process?
  • Can the clinical champion articulate the economic case to finance and procurement without us in the room?
  • Have we mapped the full decision chain, including stakeholders beyond the clinicians who attended the demo?
  • Is our distributor strategy aligned with how this hospital or hospital group actually buys?
  • What does a realistic conversion timeline look like in this market?

These questions separate activity from progress. They also reduce the risk of repeating the same cycle of strong demos followed by silent pipelines.

Commercial question to ask

If this next demo goes perfectly, what is the concrete path from clinical interest to a signed purchase order — and who needs to be involved at each step?

The signal worth following

Hospital demos remain essential for MedTech, medical device and IVD companies entering Asia-Pacific markets. They create the clinical credibility that later commercial conversations depend on.

The signal worth following is not the quality of the demo itself. It is whether the commercial pathway after the demo is clear: who decides, when budget is available, how procurement works, and how distributors support or hinder conversion.

When that pathway is designed deliberately, demos become the start of customer adoption rather than the high point of interest that never becomes a purchase order.

A question worth asking before your next hospital demo

Before investing time and resources in another clinical demonstration, ask yourself one question.

“If this demo goes perfectly, what is the realistic path from clinical interest to a signed purchase order — and who needs to be involved at each step?”

The answer often changes how the demo is planned, who is invited, and what materials are prepared.

That is where commercial conversion begins.
