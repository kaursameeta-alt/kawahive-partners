Registered MedTech Product? How to Assess APAC Market Demand

For founders with a registered MedTech or medical device product or service who need to test where demand exists and what must happen before customer interest becomes an order.

12 October 2026 · 7-minute read · KawaHive & Partners

A registered product is an important milestone. It does not, by itself, establish that the right customers know about it, that a hospital has a reason to buy, or that a distributor can generate repeat demand. Before investing further in an APAC market, founders need evidence about the customer, the buying process and the route to market.

This article sets out a practical way to assess market demand for a registered MedTech or medical device product or service. It is a go-to-market planning framework, not a market-size estimate or a substitute for local regulatory advice.

1. Start with a specific customer problem

Define the clinical, workflow or operational problem the product is intended to address. Avoid beginning with a broad country list or a product-feature checklist. Identify the care setting and the type of account where the problem is most relevant.

  • Which clinical application or workflow is the product designed to support?
  • Which hospitals, clinics or care providers are most likely to experience the problem?
  • How do those customers address it today?
  • What would make them consider changing their current approach?

Separate a stated interest in the technology from evidence that the problem is important enough to justify a purchase.

2. Map the people involved in the buying decision

The person who uses or evaluates a medical device may not control the budget or approve the purchase. Map the stakeholders relevant to the product and account. These may include clinical users, department heads, biomedical engineering, procurement, finance and senior management.

For each priority account, record who has a clinical need, who can champion the product, who assesses technical requirements, who controls the budget and who authorises the purchase. Identify missing relationships rather than treating a successful demonstration as proof that the buying process is complete.

3. Understand what must happen before an order

Work backwards from the purchase decision. Ask what evidence, approvals and internal steps are required before the organisation can issue a purchase order.

  • Is there an identified budget or a route to secure one?
  • What clinical, technical or operational evaluation is required?
  • Are procurement specifications or tender requirements involved?
  • Is there an incumbent product or supplier?
  • Who owns the next step, and when will it happen?

Document the evidence available for each opportunity. Label assumptions and verbal reports clearly. A hospital meeting, a positive user comment or a completed demonstration is a signal to investigate—not a confirmed order forecast.

4. Test distributor capability against the target customer

A distributor appointment does not automatically create market access or customer demand. Assess the partner against the accounts and buying stakeholders that matter for the product.

  • Can the distributor reach the relevant hospitals and decision-makers?
  • Does the team understand the product's clinical application and value proposition?
  • Can it coordinate demonstrations, evaluations, quotations and follow-up?
  • Can it support installation, training and after-sales requirements where relevant?
  • Does it report customer-level progress, not only stock placed or opening orders?

Ask for evidence of activity at account level. Distinguish distributor inventory from end-customer sell-through, and distinguish a stated opportunity from a documented buying process.

5. Compare the product's value with current alternatives

Customers compare a proposed solution with the way they work today, including existing equipment, competing products and the option of doing nothing. Identify the factors that influence the decision in the relevant care setting.

These may include clinical suitability, workflow fit, ease of use, service coverage, training, implementation requirements, total cost and the availability of budget. Avoid relying on generic claims such as better quality or advanced technology. Connect each value statement to a customer need and evidence the buyer can assess.

6. Prioritise markets and accounts using evidence

Do not treat every country, distributor or hospital lead as equally attractive. Compare opportunities using consistent criteria, such as the relevance of the customer problem, access to buying stakeholders, clarity of the purchasing process, channel capability, competitive alternatives and the resources needed to progress.

Keep the evidence visible. A simple record can show the account, customer need, stakeholders, current alternative, buying steps, evidence received, unresolved questions, next action and owner. This makes it easier to distinguish real progress from activity that has not moved the buying decision forward.

7. Decide what to do next

Use the evidence to make one of three decisions:

Proceed: The customer need is relevant, the buying process is understood well enough to take the next step, and there is a credible route to reach and support the account.

Validate: The opportunity may be attractive, but a critical assumption remains untested. Identify the missing evidence, the person who can provide it and the next action required.

Redirect or stop: The customer need, buying conditions, competitive position or distributor capability does not currently justify further investment. Reconsider the segment, account, value proposition or route to market.

Set a review date and define what evidence would change the decision. This prevents opportunities from remaining indefinitely in a pipeline because a stakeholder once expressed interest.

From market interest to customer orders

A stronger APAC go-to-market plan starts with the customer and works backwards from the buying decision. It connects the clinical or operational need to the people who influence the purchase, the evidence procurement requires, the alternatives already in place and the distributor's ability to execute.

The objective is not to maximise meetings or market coverage. It is to focus time and resources on the opportunities with a credible path to customer orders—and to recognise early when the evidence points elsewhere.
