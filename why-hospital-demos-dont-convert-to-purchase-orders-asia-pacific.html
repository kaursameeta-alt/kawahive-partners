Why Aren’t My Hospital Demos Converting into Purchase Orders in Asia-Pacific?

For founders with a registered product in the MedTech and medical devices sector whose hospital demonstrations are not converting into purchase orders.

29 September 2026 · 5-minute read · KawaHive & Partners

The hospital demo went well. Clinicians asked thoughtful questions. A senior doctor offered to champion the product.

If your hospital demos in Asia-Pacific aren’t turning into purchase orders, the problem is often the buying pathway, not the product.

You left thinking: We’re getting somewhere.

Six months later, there’s still no purchase order.

The product works. Clinicians see its value. But demos, feedback and evaluations have not led to orders.

It is tempting to add another pilot, paper, distributor conversation or local hire. First ask: will it move a buyer closer to a decision?

A situation from prior industry experience

Before KawaHive was established, a medical device market-entry project in an APAC market formed part of the professional experience behind this practice. The market reference has been generalised; no company, hospital, clinician or distributor is named. This example is drawn from our team's previous industry experience, before KawaHive existed. It is not a KawaHive client engagement. Hospital demonstrations were booked, clinicians were engaged, and the distributor was optimistic.

The project team spent months pursuing hospitals that already had suppliers they trusted. Every evaluation seemed to end the same way:

The clinical feedback was positive, but that interest did not progress into a confirmed buying decision.

The team did not secure a purchase order.

The clinicians’ interest was genuine. But established suppliers already had the relationships, workflows and purchasing history, so those evaluations weren’t changing the hospital’s buying situation.

The project team then stepped back from the original approach and explored adjacent clinical applications. Over the next 12 months, the team spoke with clinicians, distributors and customers. The product didn’t change; the market strategy did.

When the company entered a different clinical segment, hospital demos began leading to buying discussions. Procurement came into view earlier, and tender specifications were eventually written around the product’s strengths.

That experience continues to shape how this practice approaches customer priorities, buying pathways and go-to-market execution in Asia-Pacific.

A positive evaluation is not the same as a buying pathway. Sometimes the product isn’t what needs to change. The route to market does.

When clinical evidence feels like progress

When a respected clinician says, “I’d love to publish a paper on this,” it feels like validation. It may be valuable. But ask: What decision will this information help someone make?

A paper can strengthen credibility without bringing a purchase any closer. A clinical champion may open doors, but they may not control the budget or the buying process.

Clinical evidence matters. So does knowing who needs to be persuaded, what they need to see, and what must happen between an evaluation and a purchase order. Clinical data is most useful when it helps the people involved in the purchase make a decision.

A good hospital demo is only the beginning

Hospitals don’t buy because one clinician says yes. They buy through a hospital procurement process involving multiple people, decisions and budgets.

The clinician may use the product. Department leaders build the case; technical teams assess requirements; procurement manages the process; and finance or hospital leadership approves the spend. Budgets may already be committed. Tender windows may be limited. Existing supplier contracts may still be active. Local service may be a prerequisite.

After a good hospital demo, don’t stop at “They liked it.” Find out who needs to be involved, what they need to decide, and when. Those answers help you tell the difference between interest and a credible path to purchase.

Before you enter an APAC market, appoint a distributor or hire locally

Entering a country, appointing a distributor, hiring locally or launching another pilot all require time and capital. Before committing, pressure-test the go-to-market assumption behind the move: Will this bring a buyer closer?

For a new market, learn how the hospital buying process works in that country and clinical segment. Public hospitals may use annual or multi-year tender cycles; private groups may follow different approval processes. Regulatory approval timelines and local service expectations shape what’s possible from a go-to-market perspective. A strategy that worked in Europe or the US won’t automatically transfer to Asia-Pacific.

For a distributor, look beyond introductions and demos. Can they take an opportunity through procurement, installation, training and ongoing support?

For a local hire, ask whether you’re scaling a buying pathway that already works—or adding cost to a process that still depends on the founder.

And before launching another pilot, ask: Who will be closer to issuing a purchase order because this pilot happened? If the answer isn’t clear, consider what the pilot needs to prove—and who needs to act on the result.

An evaluation shows interest. A purchase order shows willingness to buy. Repeat purchases suggest you’re building a route to market you can scale.

Country examples: where to check procurement requirements

Public-sector purchasing routes differ by country and institution. These official sources illustrate the types of local checks a supplier should make; they do not replace the tender documents or the hospital's own procurement guidance.

  • Malaysia: The Medical Device Authority's Circular Letter No. 1/2026 describes requirements for medical-device suppliers participating in tenders for healthcare facility institutions, including establishment licensing, GDPMD, product registration and a distributor appointment letter. Review the MDA circular and the Ministry of Health tender notices.
  • Singapore: Government procurement opportunities can be searched on GeBIZ. The Ministry of Finance explains the quotation and tender routes, procurement-value thresholds and approval controls in its procurement process guidance.
  • New South Wales, Australia: HealthShare NSW Procurement Services describes central purchasing and statewide contracts that include medical equipment, as well as the supplier portal used for tender opportunities.

For the account in front of you, confirm the actual route: tender, quotation, panel or contract, private-group approval, or another purchasing pathway. Identify who owns the clinical evaluation, technical review, budget and final purchase decision.

A five-step hospital buying pathway

After a positive evaluation, use these questions to find the gap between clinical interest and a purchase decision:

  1. Clinical interest: Who sees the value, and what problem do they need the product to solve?
  2. Buying process: Who else must assess, recommend or approve the purchase?
  3. Purchase conditions: What budget, clinical data, tender, technical, service or training requirements remain?
  4. Accountable next step: Who will do what, and by when? Record an owner and date rather than “follow up”.
  5. Buying signals: What observable event would show progress, such as a procurement meeting, quote request, confirmed budget or tender inclusion?

Not every evaluation should convert. A useful outcome may be learning that the budget, timing or clinical case is not strong enough. Record what you learned and decide whether to continue, pause or redirect effort.

How to tell hospital interest from a real purchase order

Clinical interest is valuable. Purchase orders are the clearest buying signal.

The challenge isn’t to dismiss clinical enthusiasm or clinical findings. It’s to connect them to a repeatable path from evaluation to procurement, and from procurement to paid adoption—in the market you’re entering.

If you’ve run dozens of hospital demos without purchase orders, you may not need more demos yet. Start by understanding what’s blocking the buying decision: budget timing, tender requirements, an incumbent supplier, distributor capability or something else.

Frequently asked questions

Why do hospital demos not lead to purchase orders?

A positive clinical evaluation may not resolve budget ownership, procurement requirements, tender timing, incumbent supplier arrangements or service concerns. Identify who makes the buying decision and agree a dated next step that moves the account toward purchase.

How long does hospital procurement take in Asia-Pacific?

There is no single timeline across Asia-Pacific. Timing depends on the country, hospital type, budget cycle, tender rules, device category and required approvals, so validate the process with the specific hospital and local channel.

Should I add another pilot?

Only if the pilot answers a defined question that matters to the buying decision. Before starting, agree what will be tested, who will assess the result, and what decision or next step follows.
